You need backup power and you have a hard budget ceiling. You have also probably seen the scary stories — cheap generators that failed in an outage, or "budget" machines that turned out to be anything but.

The good news: a tight budget does not force a bad decision. It just means you have to be smart about where the money goes.

## Where to spend, and where to save

Not every part of a generator deserves your money. Think of it this way:

- **Don't overspend on the engine badge.** You need a reliable, serviceable engine — not a premium import. A mature, mass-produced domestic engine (like a Shangchai/SDEC unit) runs just as well for ordinary standby duty.
- **Don't skimp on the invisible parts.** The automatic transfer switch, the cabling, the controller, and proper commissioning — these are what decide whether the set actually works when you need it.

Redirect the money you save on the badge into those. A well-installed budget engine beats a badly-installed fancy one.

## The mistake "cheap" buyers make

The trap is buying the cheapest thing on a platform with no support behind it. A generator is not a phone. You need someone who will size it correctly, deliver it, set it up, and answer the phone a year later. That service costs a little — and saves a lot.

## What a budget buyer should ask

| Your question | What you should demand |
|---|---|
| Is the engine reliable? | A mature, widely-serviced, mass-produced model |
| Are parts cheap and available? | Common consumables, locally reachable |
| What's included? | Transfer switch, controller, commissioning |
| Will it be supported? | A supplier who stands behind the set |

## When budget does not justify the cheapest

There is one situation where you should not pinch pennies: if the set will run as continuous prime power many hours a day, or protect a critical facility. Then step up a class. But for typical standby duty — a factory, workshop, shop, farm — a properly specified, mature-engine set on a sensible budget is the right, rational call.

## Engineering checklist before you order

Before choosing a generator, collect the exact connected load, the largest motor kW, the site altitude, ambient temperature and the ATS logic you need. A unit sized too small will trip under step load; a unit sized too large will run at low load, wet-stack and waste fuel. Ask the supplier for a one-hour factory load test report, not just a nameplate photo.
