When your backup need climbs past a few hundred kW, a new question appears that smaller buyers never face: should I buy one big generator, or several smaller ones running together? And how do I know if I even need to think about this yet?

Let's cut through it.

## First: do you actually need this scale?

If your building or plant needs less than ~300 kW, stop here. A single mid-size silent set is the answer. This question only starts to matter when you are protecting something where downtime is not an inconvenience — a hospital's critical areas, a data hall, a continuous production line.

If that is you, read on.

## Why one engine is sometimes not enough

A single 800 kW set works — until the day it needs servicing, or something faults. At that moment, your whole facility is depending on one machine. That is a single point of failure, and mission-critical sites cannot accept it.

The standard answer is to run **several units in parallel**:

- **Redundancy** — if one set is down, the others still carry the load.
- **Growth** — as you expand, you add another unit instead of replacing the plant.
- **Efficiency** — running fewer, better-loaded units uses less fuel than one oversize engine.

## "How do I even know my loads?"

This is where a buyer usually gets stuck. The right move is to gather your **load schedule** — everything the generator has to carry, especially the big switch-on events like chillers and motor banks. When the grid fails, those hit the generator all at once as a sudden "step". A heavy-duty, high-power engine like an 800 kW Mitsubishi block accepts that step without bogging down; a weaker one will stumble.

That is the detail that decides whether your backup actually works on the day.

## The honest warning

Do not buy a megawatt-class set for a shop floor. You will pay far more than you need. And do not assume one unit is safe for a hospital or data centre — that is the gamble.
